In a life-threatening emergency, call your local emergency number first. This guide helps you act while help is on the way.

🫀 CPR (adult)

Call emergency services (or have someone call) and get an AED if available.
  1. Check response. Tap the shoulders and shout. If no response and no normal breathing, start CPR.
  2. Position your hands. Heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, other hand on top, fingers interlocked, arms straight.
  3. Push hard and fast. Compress at least 5 cm (2 in) deep, 100–120 times per minute. Let the chest fully rise between pushes. Use the metronome below.
  4. Rescue breaths (if trained). After 30 compressions, tilt the head back, lift the chin, give 2 breaths. If untrained, do continuous compressions only.
  5. Don't stop. Continue until the person breathes, an AED is ready, or professionals take over. Swap with another person every 2 minutes if you can.

Compression metronome — push on every beat (110 beats/min)

PUSH

Don't be afraid of pushing too hard — broken ribs heal; a stopped heart doesn't restart on its own.

🫁 Choking (adult / child over 1 year)

If the person cannot breathe, cough or speak, act immediately and have someone call emergency services.
  1. Encourage coughing. If they can still cough or speak, let them keep coughing — don't slap yet.
  2. 5 back blows. Lean them forward and give 5 sharp blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
  3. 5 abdominal thrusts. Stand behind them, fist just above the navel, grasp with the other hand, pull sharply inward and upward 5 times.
  4. Repeat. Alternate 5 back blows and 5 thrusts until the object comes out or the person collapses.
  5. If they collapse: start CPR and check the mouth for the object before each set of breaths.

Don't do abdominal thrusts on infants under 1 year (use back blows and chest thrusts) or on pregnant people (use chest thrusts).

🩸 Severe bleeding

Call emergency services for any heavy or spurting bleed.
  1. Press hard on the wound. Use a clean cloth, bandage or even a shirt. Direct, firm, continuous pressure is what stops bleeding.
  2. Don't peek. If blood soaks through, add more layers on top — never remove the first layer.
  3. Raise the limb above heart level if the wound is on an arm or leg and no fracture is suspected.
  4. Embedded object? Do not pull it out. Press around it and pad it in place.
  5. Watch for shock. Pale, cold, clammy, faint — lay the person down, keep them warm, keep pressing.

Tourniquets are a last resort for life-threatening limb bleeding that pressure can't control — note the time you applied it.

🧠 Stroke — think F.A.S.T.

Every minute costs brain. Call emergency services at the first sign — do not wait to see if it passes.
  1. F — Face. Ask them to smile. Does one side of the face droop?
  2. A — Arms. Ask them to raise both arms. Does one drift down or fail to lift?
  3. S — Speech. Ask them to repeat a simple sentence. Is it slurred or strange?
  4. T — Time. Any one sign means call emergency services NOW, and note the exact time symptoms started — treatment decisions depend on it.
  5. While waiting: keep them still and calm, lying with head and shoulders slightly raised. Nothing to eat or drink.

Don't give aspirin for a suspected stroke — some strokes are bleeds, and aspirin makes those worse.

⚡ Seizure

Call emergency services if the seizure lasts over 5 minutes, repeats, follows an injury, or it's their first one.
  1. Protect, don't restrain. Clear hard objects away and put something soft under the head.
  2. Time it. Start a timer the moment it begins.
  3. Nothing in the mouth. They cannot swallow their tongue — putting objects in the mouth causes injury.
  4. Afterwards: roll them into the recovery position, stay with them, speak calmly. Confusion after a seizure is normal.

Don't hold the person down and don't give food or water until they are fully alert.

🔥 Burns

Call emergency services for burns that are large, deep, on the face/hands/genitals, or caused by chemicals or electricity.
  1. Stop the burning. Remove the person from the source. For chemical burns, brush off dry chemical first, then flush.
  2. Cool with running water for at least 20 minutes. Cool water — not ice.
  3. Remove jewellery and loose clothing near the burn before it swells — but never peel off anything stuck to the skin.
  4. Cover loosely with cling film or a clean, non-fluffy cloth.

No ice, no butter, no toothpaste, no creams, and never burst blisters.

🛌 Recovery position

For someone unconscious but breathing normally. Call emergency services, then position them.
  1. Kneel beside them. Place the arm nearest you at a right angle, palm up.
  2. Far arm across the chest, back of their hand against their near cheek — hold it there.
  3. Bend the far knee up, then pull it toward you to roll them onto their side.
  4. Tilt the head back slightly to keep the airway open; adjust the top leg to stop them rolling.
  5. Check breathing regularly until help arrives. If breathing stops, roll them onto their back and start CPR.

Don't leave them on their back — the tongue or vomit can block the airway.

🌡️ Heatstroke

Hot skin plus confusion, collapse or seizures is heatstroke — a true emergency. Call for help immediately.
  1. Move to shade or a cool room. Remove excess clothing.
  2. Cool aggressively. Soak with cool water, fan them, put cold packs on neck, armpits and groin. Immersion in cool water is best if available.
  3. If fully alert, give small sips of water — nothing if drowsy or confused.
  4. If unconscious but breathing, use the recovery position and keep cooling.

Don't give paracetamol or aspirin — they don't help heatstroke and can cause harm.

🐝 Anaphylaxis (severe allergic reaction)

Swelling of face/throat, wheezing, hives spreading fast, collapse — call emergency services immediately.
  1. Use their adrenaline auto-injector (EpiPen) NOW if they have one — outer mid-thigh, hold 3 seconds. Don't wait to "see how it goes".
  2. Position: lying down with legs raised; sitting up if breathing is hard. Never stand them up suddenly.
  3. Second dose after 5 minutes if there's no improvement and a second injector is available.
  4. Even if they recover, they must go to hospital — reactions can return hours later.

Antihistamines alone do not treat anaphylaxis — adrenaline is the only first-line treatment.
